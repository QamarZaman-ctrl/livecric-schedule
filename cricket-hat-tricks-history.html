The Ultimate Guide to Famous Hat-Tricks in Cricket History

June 2026 By QZ Admin

In international cricket, certain achievements stand out as true testaments to individual bowling mastery[cite: 11]. While scoring a century or taking a five-wicket haul represents exceptional performance, there is one rare bowling feat that instantly electrifies a stadium and captures global headlines: the hat-trick[cite: 11]. It is a moment of pure theatrical drama where a single bowler turns a match completely on its head within the span of three consecutive deliveries[cite: 11].

When broken down into its core definition, a hat-trick signifies the extraordinary feat of a single bowler dismissing three different batsmen on three completely consecutive deliveries within a single match[cite: 11]. The official laws state that the three wickets must be successive legal balls, though they do not necessarily have to occur within the same over[cite: 11].

Critical Hat-Trick Rules and Regulations

The International Cricket Council (ICC) and the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC) enforce highly specific technical parameters governing this milestone[cite: 11]:

  • The Continuity Clause: The three deliveries must be the bowler's consecutive legal balls[cite: 11]. If a bowler takes wickets on balls 5 and 6 of an over, and then claims another on the first ball of their next over, the hat-trick is perfectly valid[cite: 11].
  • Spanning Across Innings: Under official laws, a hat-trick can span two separate innings or days of a match[cite: 11]. A bowler can take a wicket with the final ball of an opponent's first innings, and return to strike with their first two deliveries in the second innings[cite: 11]. Australia's Merv Hughes famously spread a hat-trick across three separate overs and two different days[cite: 11]!
  • The Impact of Extras: If a bowler delivers a wide or no-ball between wickets, the consecutive chain of legal balls is disrupted[cite: 11]. A wicket on the delivery immediately following the extra ball does not count as a hat-trick[cite: 11].
  • Valid Modes of Dismissal: To count, the dismissal must be directly credited to the bowler's record (Bowled, Caught, LBW, Stumped, or Hit Wicket)[cite: 11]. Crucially, a Run-Out does not count toward a bowler's sequence because it is credited to the fielding team[cite: 11].

The Victorian Origin: How the Term Was Coined

The term was coined in England during the mid-19th century, belonging exclusively to cricket long before it was adopted by football or hockey[cite: 11]. In 1858, a bowler named H.H. Stephenson took three wickets on three consecutive deliveries at Sheffield[cite: 11]. To honor this unprecedented feat, enthusiastic spectators held a public collection and used the money to purchase a physical top hat, which was presented to Stephenson as a formal prize[cite: 11].

While Stephenson's feat occurred before official Test matches, the record for the first international hat-trick belongs to Australia's Fred Spofforth, known as "The Demon," who dismantled England at the Melbourne Cricket Ground (MCG) on January 2, 1879[cite: 11].

Format-by-Format Historical Highlights

Red-Ball Test Excellence

The list of Test cricket hat-tricks is the most exclusive club in the sport, with fewer than 50 recorded over 150 years[cite: 11]. Iconic entries include Shane Warne’s 1994 Ashes hat-trick at the MCG, Harbhajan Singh’s legendary 2001 burst against Australia in Kolkata, and Peter Siddle's historic birthday hat-trick at the Gabba in 2010[cite: 11]. Taking a hat-trick on Test debut is an even rarer statistical miracle, achieved only by Maurice Allom (1929), Peter Petherick (1976), and Alomin Islam (2003)[cite: 11].

Limited-Overs formats (ODI & T20I)

The first-ever ODI hat-trick was executed by Pakistan's pace icon Jalal-ud-Din against Australia in 1982[cite: 11]. In the explosive modern era, Australia's Brett Lee secured the first T20I hat-trick against Bangladesh during the inaugural 2007 ICC T20 World Cup, setting off a rapid expansion of short-format bowling milestones[cite: 11].

The Elite Double Hat-Trick Phenomenon

Many newcomers mistakenly believe that a double hat-trick means taking six wickets in six balls[cite: 11]. However, under official international definitions, a double hat-trick refers specifically to a bowler taking four wickets in four consecutive balls[cite: 11].

It is called a double hat-trick because a four-ball sequence mathematically contains two distinct, overlapping sets of three consecutive wickets[cite: 11]:

$$\text{Ball Sequence: } B_1, B_2, B_3, B_4$$
$$\text{Outcomes: } W_1, W_2, W_3, W_4$$
  • Hat-Trick #1: Achieved across balls $B_1, B_2, \text{ and } B_3$[cite: 11].
  • Hat-Trick #2: Achieved across overlapping balls $B_2, B_3, \text{ and } B_4$[cite: 11].

This legendary international four-in-four club features only Lasith Malinga (who achieved it twice), spin master Rashid Khan, women's pioneer Anuradha Doddaballapur, and West Indies' Jason Holder[cite: 11].

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1: Who secured the first-ever hat-trick in a Cricket World Cup?
India's Chetan Sharma took the first-ever World Cup hat-trick in 1987, clean-bowling three New Zealand batsmen[cite: 11]. Other iconic World Cup entries include Saqlain Mushtaq (1999) and Mohammed Shami (2019)[cite: 11].
Q2: Who was the first pioneer to claim a hat-trick in women's cricket?
The first-ever women's Test match hat-trick was taken by Australia's legendary Betty Wilson in 1958 against England[cite: 11]. In modern formats, stars like Ellyse Perry and Megan Schutt continue the legacy of excellence[cite: 11].
Q3: Does a hat-trick add runs or wickets to a player's official career statistics?
Yes, standard match hat-tricks add full credits to a player's official international stats[cite: 11]. However, any wickets taken during a tiebreaker Super Over are completely excluded from a player's official metrics[cite: 10].
